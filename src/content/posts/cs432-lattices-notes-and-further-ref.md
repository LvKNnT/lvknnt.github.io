# 1.1 Notes and further references
The video series *Post-QUantum Cryptography Explained ([https://cryptography101.ca/pqc](https://cryptography101.ca/pqc)) provides an elementary introduction to post-quantum cryptography and lattice-base cryptography. 

Lattices are fundamental structures that play a central role in numerous areas of mathematics and computer science, including the geometry of numbers, coding theory, optimization, and cryptography. The *geometry of numbers* is a branch of number theory that applies geometric techniques, particularly those involving lattices, to problems concerning integers and algebraic numbers. The subject was founded by Hermann Minkowski in the late nineteenth century; a classical reference is the book by Cassels. Notable applications of lattices include H. Lenstra's polynomial-time algorithm for integer linear programming in fixed dimension $n$, Viazovska's solution of the sphere packing problem in dimension 8, the work of Kannnan and Lovász on the covering minima of convex bodies, and the capacity-achieving lattice coding scheme for the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) channel developed by Erez and Zamir. More recently, in 2023, Reis and Rothvoss introduced a $(\log{(2n)}) ^ {O(n)}$-time randomized algorithm for solving integer programs in $n$ variables. 

Kyber and Dilithium were standardized by NIST in August 2024 as ML-KEM (FIPS 203) and ML-DSA (FIPS 204), respectively, whereas Falcon is being standardized as FN-DSA in FIPS 206, which at the time of writing has not yet been finalized. FordoKEM was standardized by ISO in June 2026. These notes focus on developing a thorough understanding of Kyber, Dilithium and FrodoKEM, and providing background information to appreciate Falcon. As part of its post-quantum cryptography standardization initiative, NIST has also finalized standards for the stateful hash-based signature schemes LMS and XMSS in SP 800-208, as well as the stateless hash-based signature scheme SPHINCS+ in FIPS 205. Menezes, van Oorschot and Preneel give an overview of these schemes

# 2.6 Notes and further references 
The first monograph devoted to algorithmic problems in lattices and their computational complexity is the 2002 book by Micciancio and Goldwasser. Part IV of Galbraith's book introduces lattices and explores their applications in cryptanalysis. Nguyen explains the connections between the Hermite constants, lattice algorithms, and sphere packings. Peikert provides a comprehensive survey of the major developments in lattice-based cryptography up to 2015, including fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) and attribute-based encryption (ABE). More recently, Lyubashevsky offers a detailed exposition of the mathematical foundations and design principles underlying SIS- nad LWE-based cryptosystems, including Kyber and Dilithium. Kyber and Dilithium are fully specified in the NIST standards FIPS 203 and FIPS 204

Conway and Sloane's book is a rich source of information on the Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$. The basis matrix for $\Lambda_{24}$ presented in Example 2.13 is taken from Figure 4.12 of that book. Bonnecaze and Solé gave a construction of the Leech lattice from the extended quaternary Golay code of length 24 over $\mathbb{Z}_4$. The *sphere packing* problem is to find an arrangement of non-overlapping unit balls in $\mathbb{R}^{24}$ is attained with unit balls centered at the points of the Leech lattice. This work builds on Viazovska's proof that the densest sphere packing in $\mathbb{R}^8$ is attained by placing unit balls at the points of the scaled lattice $\sqrt{2} E_8$ (the $E_8$ lattice is defined in Exercise 10.11)

In 1998, Ajtai proved that SVP is NP-hard under randomized reductions. Recently, in 2026, Wan removed the randomness fromm Ajtai's reduction, finally establishing the NP-hardness of SVP. Khot proved that the approximate version $\operatorname{SVP}_\gamma$ remains NP-hard ynder randomized reductions for constant approximate factors $\gamma$. The claim that $\operatorname{SVP}_\gamma$ is unlikely to be NP=hard for $\gamma > \sqrt{n}$ is supported by a result of Aharonov and Regev that there exists $c > 0$ for which $\operatorname{GapSVP}_{c \sqrt{n}}$ lies in NP $\cap$ coNP ($\operatorname{GapSVP}_\beta$ is a decisional version of SVP). Blomer and Seifert proved that $\operatorname{SIVP}_\gamma$ is NP-hard for constant approximate factors $\gamma$. Laarhoven, van de Pol and de Weger and Stephens Dabidowitz provide comprehensive overviews of the relationships between the main lattice problem. Stephens-Davidowitz gave a proof that $\operatorname{SIVP}_{\gamma \sqrt{n}} \le \operatorname{SVP}_\gamma$ for every $\gamma \ge 1$

Babai inntroduced two conceptually simple polynomial-time algorithm for $\operatorname{SVP}_\gamma$. His *nearest algorithm* (see Exercise 12.5) attains $\gamma = 1 + 2n (\frac{9}{2}) ^ {n / 2}$. The nearst plan algorithm has found applications in several ares beyond cryptography. In particular, Chen et al. showed in 2025 that GPTQ, a widely used method for quantizing the weights of large language models (LLMs), can be viewed as an application of the nearest plane algorithm 

Gao constructed a family of lattices for which the Bounded Distance Decoding (BDD) problem (Definition 4.19) is NP-hard but solvable in polynomial time on average, demonstrating that worst-case NP-hardness of a lattice problem does not imply its aversge-case hardness.

# 5.4 Notes and further references
Ring-SIS, an analogue of SIS that employs certain polynomial rinngs, was formulated by Lyubashevsky and Micciancio. The module-based analogue, Module-SIS (MSIS), which employs vectors of polynomials, was developed by Langlois and Stehlé. 

Ring-LWE, a variant of LWE that employs certain polynomial rings, was formulated by Lyubashevsky, Peikert and Regev, who also provided a wrost-case to average-case quantum reduction from approx-SIVP in ideal lattices to Ring-LWE. The module variant, Module-LWE (MLWE), was first introduced by Brakerski, Gentry and Vaikuntanathan, and a worst-case to average-case quantum redution was developed by Langlois and Stehlé. A concrete analysis of the tightness gaps in the Ring-LWE and MLWE reductions was carried out by Koblitz, Samajder, Sarkar and Singha, who argued that the approximation factor in the SIVP problem is too large to yield meaningful security guarantess in practice. Wenger et al. provide concrete benchmarks for several lattice-based attacks on LWE with parameter choices from Kyber and a Ring-LWE-based homorphic encryption scheme

Kyber-PKE was developed by Bos et al. The fujisaki-Okamoto transform was introduced in 1999. A variant of it, due to Hofheinz, Hovelmanns and Kiltz, was used by Bos et al. in the design of Kyber-KEM. For a comprehensive description of Kyber-KEM, including the specifications of the hash functions $G, H$, and $J$, the eXtendable Output Function (XOF) used to generate $A$, and the pseudorandom function (PRF) used to generate $s, e, r, e_1, e_2$ see FIPS 203. Examples of Kyber-KEM for the three standardized parameter sets can be found at the CrypTool-Online website

D'Anvers et al. showed how information leaked through Kyber-KEM decapsulation failure can be exploited to formulate an MLWE instance with a secret that is smaller than the original pair $s, e$, thereby making the problem easier to solve. They then applied standard cost estimates for solving MLWE to approximate the time required to recover the secret key. Their analysis inndicates that the attack is ineffective when the number of decapsulation queries is limited to $2 ^ {64}$. In subsequent work, D'Anvers, Rossi and Virdia proposed a technique called *derectional failure boosting*, in which a previously identified failing ciphertext is leveraged to dramatically accelerate the discovery of additional ciphertexts that also fail decapsulation

Side-channel attacks against Kyber implementations continue to be an active area of research. A comprehensive survey on side-channel attacks on Kyber implementations was presented in 2024 by Rabi, Chattopadhyay, D'Anvers and Baksi. Recent notable contributions include the works of Hermelink, Ning, Petri and Strieder, Bernstein et al., and Fouque, Marion, Nguyen and Wallet

The security of Kyber and Dilithium rests on the hardness of problems in *structured* lattices : as explained in $\S 5$, the lattices underlying the Module-LWE and Module-SIS problems possess considerable structure in that their basis matrices consist of anti-circulant blocks. FrodoKEM, the key encapsulation mechanism described in $\S 9$, takes a defferent approach, basing its security on the hardness of LWE in *unstructured* lattices. The absence of algebraic strucutre in FrodoKEM's lattices affords a more conservative security guarantee than that of Kyber, but comes at the considerable cost of larger public keys and ciphertexts, slower encapsulation, and slower decapsulation

Most PQC deployments adopts a *hybrid* approach. In this model, traditional elliptic curve key agreement such as elliptic-curve Diffle-Hellman (ECDH) is combined with a quantum-resistant key establishment mechaism such as Kyber-KEM. The final shared secret key is derived by combining the outputs of both schemes via a key derivation fucntion. Hybrid constructions mitigate *harvest-now, decrypt late* attacks by ensuring that recorded traffic remains secure even if large-scale quantum computers become available in the future. At the same time, the inclusion of a classical and well-studied primitive such as ECDH provides a hedge against the possibility that unforeseen weaknesses are later discovered in the quantum-resistant component. This dual protection, however, is lost if cryptographically relevant quantum computers become available *and* major weaknesses are discovered in the quantum-resistant scheme

Giacon, Heuer and Poettering studied key combiners for hybrid key agreement that remain secure provided that at least one of the constituent KEMs is secure. NIST's SP 800-227 recommends the following generic key combiner. For $i \in \{1, 2\}$, let $ek_i$, $c_i$ and $K_i$ denote the encapsulation key, ciphertext and shared secret key for two KEMs. The combined key is then computed as $K = H(K_1, K_2, c_1, c_2, ek_1, ek_2, \text{domain_sep})$, where "domain_sep" is a string that uniquely identifies the composite KEM scheme in use. A hybrid KEM tailored to combine X25519 elliptic curve key agreement with ML-KEM-768 was proposed by Barbosa et al.; this instruction achieves improved efficiency compared to generic combiners. The IETF has standardized several methods for hybrid key agreement, including RFC 9954, RFC 100024, and RFC 10042.
